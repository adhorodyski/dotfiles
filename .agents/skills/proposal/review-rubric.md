# tl;dr - The Hourglass Shape

A great problem/solution statement is shaped like an "hourglass":

- **Strategy** – Quick overview of the subset of the strategy that is improved by this proposal.

- **Background** – Thick and juicy explanation of the current state of the world, without hinting at what the problem is. It should not include the problem or solution.

- **Problem** – An extremely short (ideally, single sentence) statement articulating the exact problem in the clearest language possible, following the format:
  **Problem:** When <preconditions>, if <action>, then <impact>.

- **Solution** – A detailed explanation of how we solve this problem.

The format looks like:

> **Proposal: <single memorable line>**
>
> **Strategy:** <brief description>
>
> **Background:** <detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail>
>
> **Problem:** When <preconditions>, if <action>, then <impact>.
>
> **Solution:** <detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail detail>

# Why this matters

It doesn't matter how individually talented and ambitious you are, your success in life will ultimately boil down to how effectively you collaborate -- and [the quality of your collaboration is directly determined by your humility](https://sites.google.com/a/expensify.com/manifesto/home/Core-Qualities?authuser=1#h.p_F8x8E1OuXUqo). To put an even finer point on it, the single most important skill you can cultivate to achieve great things in life, by yourself and with your team, is to get really good at writing a clear problem statement.

That might seem hard to believe. But the difference between a "discussion" and an "argument" is fundamentally whether or not you agree on the problem you are trying to solve:

- If you do agree on the problem you are trying to solve, then you have some framework to objectively evaluate multiple solutions against each other. You have some ability to evaluate the priority of solving the problem, and the effectiveness of the solution. A well defined problem statement lets you estimate ROI (because the problem being solved determines the "return"). More simply: without a defined problem, what is there even to discuss?

- Rather, if you don't agree on the problem, all you _can_ do is argue. Someone says "I think we should do X", and that's just... a true statement. Yes, that person does think that. What is there to talk about? How can you disagree with that: they are just stating a personal opinion, to which everyone is entitled. Furthermore, if you don't want them to do it (or if you don't want to help them), then how is there any way to express that without directly rejecting them, personally -- not their idea, but their request as an individual. Without a defined problem statement, there can be no discussion: it's just a direct test of wills.

Once seen, it's kind of hard to "unsee" -- like when your friend gets a particular kind of car that you haven't noticed before, but then suddenly see everywhere. It was always there. You just didn't see it.

Look close and nearly every argument, especially the ones where emotions run high, can almost certainly be traced back to ambiguous problem statements, where each party is trying to solve different problems, or they are making bad assumptions about what the other party is trying to solve... or even worse (and even more common): no specific problem at all.

To avoid being "that person", do your peers a solid by just telling them exactly what problem you are trying to solve, to ensure your conversations are dynamic discussions, and not angry arguments. You owe it to them, and you owe it to yourself.

# When should I _not_ write a problem statement?

The whole point of a problem/solution statement is to engage others to help you solve a problem. So anytime you want to engage others, a crisp problem statement helps you communicate and collaborate with them effectively. However, if you are not asking anyone for help, then _you probably shouldn't do it_. If your problem:

- Primarily affects you
- Can be solved by you
- Is very low stakes
- Can be easily changed later

Then don't bother with a P/S: just do it, and tell them what you did. Even better, write a SO documenting what you did, and then share your SO with others you think might benefit from it. But don't feel obligated to start a big discussion asking for advice on what you should do. For example, an untied shoe is a problem. But it only affects you, and it's easily solved by you, so you don't need to engage others. Make sure you are saving your #whatsnext post for meaty problems that actually affect and require others.

# What is the "Strategy" section?

The way Expensify wins is by choosing a good high level strategy, and then making sure everything we do supports it. The "strategy" section is how you demonstrate that alignment. This isn't a section for creativity -- we don't want every project to have a different strategy, after all, we want everyone following the same strategy. Indeed, the more creativity required to "connect the dots" between a proposal and our strategy, the more skeptical we should be about it. Some ideas to keep in mind while writing this section:

- Consider the difference between our need to survive in the current "red ocean" versus our desire to expand into an uncontested "blue ocean":
  - If it's about the "red ocean" talk about the importance of increasing our competitiveness in an extremely difficult market.

  - If it's about the "blue ocean" talk about the importance of increasing our attractiveness to potential customers currently sitting on the sidelines.

- Consider what metric in our accounting this is trying to improve -- even if you don't state it outright (but feel free to!), this might organize your thoughts:
  - If the proposal tries to reduce the "Cost to Acquire a Customer" (CAC), perhaps talk about the importance of decreasing the cost per "lead", increasing the "conversion" of leads, or maybe accelerating the speed of conversion.

  - If the proposal tries to increase the "Life Time Value" (LTV) of a customer, talk about the importance of maximizing how many seats are active, or increasing how much we earn per seat, or even extending the lifetime of a customer to get the most lifetime revenue per customer.

  - If the proposal has no effect on new or existing customers, and is just about efficiency, perhaps talk about the importance of "Free Cash Flow" (FCF) as a tool for buybacks, or the value of profit margins as the most important long-term defensive moat.

- Focus on 1-2 benefits as much as possible. Everything helps many ways, but the point is to focus on one benefit: anything that needs to justify itself by showing a tiny improvement to 10 variables isn't nearly as powerful as something that makes a major improvement to 1 variable.

- Try to limit it to 2-4 sentences: it doesn't need to be complicated, just enough to orient the reader toward what part of our strategy this relates to.

Here are some example strategies:

- > Expensify aims to pursue a "blue ocean" strategy that brings new innovation to market at an even lower price, in order to reach smaller customers who are currently priced out or uninterested by the current options. New businesses created by young entrepreneurs are especially social, mobile, and more trusting of AI than previous generations. For this reason, we are using a chat-first, AI-powered, Concierge-based onboarding model to increase the speed and reduce the cost of sale.

- > Expensify is fighting for survival in an extremely "red ocean" of largely equivalent competitors trading customers back and forth at ever-decreasing margins. Everyone in this market is moving toward integrated product suites, both to defend their shrinking margins, as well as to reduce churn to competitors who offer more integrated functionality at the same or better price.

In particular, the strategy section is NOT a place to:

- Summarize the proposal as a whole

- Explicitly state or even hint at the specific problem

- Explicitly state or even hint at the specific solution

When you read the strategy summary, it should say things that are relevant to understanding the proposal, but don't directly hint to the proposal itself.

# How to write a good "Background" statement

Don't assume the reader already knows the relevant context, provide a "background" that does as many of the following as you think are necessary:

- Explains the current state of the product

- Explains what the customer is trying to do

- Highlights what the competition does in this situation

- Quantifies this in terms of user count or revenue

However, make sure your background gives no hint about what problem you are trying to solve (and certainly not what the solution is). The background contextualizes the problem and solution, but doesn't step on its toes.

# How to write a good "Problem" statement

A good problem statement is in this format:

- **Problem:** When <preconditions>, if <action>, then <impact>.
  - Just state the direct cause and effect, with minimal fluff and analysis.
  - **<preconditions> = Whatever is happening just before the problem occurs.** The preconditions that allow for the problem to happen.
    - Don't say _"When <the problem occurs>"_ -- explain the state of the environment that sets up or enables the problem to happen, not the problem itself.
  - **<action> = The specific action that causes the problem.** This is the person or system that did something that might generally be fine, but is not fine when the preconditions are true. Additionally, without this action, the preconditions themselves aren't necessarily problematic.
    - Don't say _"if they <don't / fail to experience the solution>"_ -- explain what action is taken that triggers the problem, not what action they _don't_ take or what effect they _don't_ receive.
  - **<impact> = The consequence of that action.** This is the undesirable outcome of doing <action> when <preconditions> are true -- it might be obvious, but state it outright.
    - Don't say _"then we <don't / fail to get benefit>"_ -- explain what actually happens, not what _doesn't_ happen.

In short, a good problem statement makes no mention whatsoever of the desired solution. This sounds obvious, but is easier said than done. The point of not mentioning the solution is to force you to identify the _actual_ problem you are trying to solve, and not wave your hands with a "reverse solution statement". For example:

- **Bad**
  - _Problem:_ We don't have a car
  - Problem: When we don't have a car, we can't do the things a car does, preventing us from doing car things.
  - _Solution:_ Buy a car.
- **Good**
  - _Problem:_ When I buy large objects from the store, then I crash my bike trying to bring them home, which puts me in the hospital.
  - _Solution:_ Buy a truck.
  - _Solution:_ Rent a truck.
  - _Solution:_ Hire movers.
  - _Solution:_ Buy smaller, bike-sized objects.

A real problem description enables a much richer discussion to find more creative solutions. Having multiple viable solutions is a good indicator that your problem statement is NOT a reverse solution description.

# How to identify a bad problem statement

Similarly, a sign that you are actually doing a reverse solution statement is if it contains phrases like:

- **Problem:** We don't do/have X; **Solution**: Build X
  - There are an infinite number of things we _don't_ do: not doing something isn't inherently a problem. You might think it is incredibly obvious why a particular deficiency is a problem when an infinite range of other deficiencies aren't -- so obvious that it doesn't need to be stated. But just state it outright. An obvious problem statement is the goal: this is not a place to hint or infer, it's a place to be clear.
    - _Protip: Rewrite the problem statement to explain the consequence of not doing/having X -- without mentioning X itself._

- **Problem**: We lack insight/knowledge/awareness/visibility; **Solution**: Create insight
  - Information isn't itself valuable. Not having it isn't inherently a problem; having it isn't itself a solution. Information is only valuable _when it enables us to act_ -- so the problem isn't the lack of information, but the problem is that there is something _we aren't doing_. Part of the solution might be getting more information -- but the information isn't the whole solution, the solution is that we are _doing_ something with that information that we weren't doing before.
    - _Protip: Rewrite the problem statement into what decision you would like to make, or what action you would like to take, that we currently can't. Then gathering data is one possible solution to that problem, but that's separate from the problem itself._

- **Problem:** There are problems A, B, C, D, and E. **Solution:** Do F.
  - Are all of A-E equally important? If one stands out, lead with it and focus on it. Otherwise, it's likely you are just "fitting the problem to the solution" and trying to invent a justification for a solution you've already mentally committed to, and aren't genuinely trying to create a collaborative, problem-focused solution.
    - _Protip: Focus on one problem at a time, to enable a collaborative conversation about each of the problems in isolation._

- **Problem:** X is inefficient/error-prone/confusing. **Solution:** Do Y.
  - Everything is inefficient and error prone. Everything is confusing and cumbersome. Without some kind of quantification, they are not problems, they are just statements of reality. Without some kind of measurable improvement, it's not a solution: it's just a change.
    - Protip: Decompose the problematic area into a handful of specific, common, important actions the user needs to take, and then highlight what is specifically preventing or complicating them in doing that.

Basically, a bad problem statement (i.e., a reverse solution statement) is written in such a fashion that it only allows for a single solution. To enable the most creative, most collaborative discussion, equip your peers with the tools to engage by listing all the key assumptions that went into your understanding of the problem, and connecting that directly to the solution.

# How to write a good solution statement

The first step in writing a good solution statement is to write a good problem statement. Without a good problem statement, the solution statement cannot be evaluated against anything -- it's just a proposal to do stuff, for reasons left unexplained and uncontestable. However, if you have a good problem statement, then:

1. **Be specific.** It's not a proposal if you aren't actually proposing anything. Don't leave the reader guessing, or hope they will imagine the same thing you are. Don't leave everything for predesign (i.e., the solution can't be "come up with a solution"): make a real proposal you think is actually pretty good and complete, and then use predesign to refine it.
   1. If you are using a new vendor, say who it is.

   2. If there are a series of steps, show them in a numbered list.

   3. If there is a new button, name it and say what happens when you press it.

   4. If there is a new screen, detail what's on it, and how you get to it.

   5. If there is a new CSV file or emailed report, say what the columns and rows are.

2. **Explain how it solves the problem.** Don't leave it implied, show precisely how with the solution in place, the problem no longer occurs.
   1. If you are creating a new report, explain who looks at that report, how often, and what specifically they do after they look at it. Data doesn't solve anything by itself, it only enables someone or something to solve the problem -- but only if you explain what that actually is.

   2. If you are creating a new feature, explain how they use it to solve the problem -- and how they will know to use it. Adding a new feature doesn't automatically cause people to discover, understand, and use it. The problem isn't solved until the user actually solves it, so include that in your solution.

3. **Solve every part of the problem.** Make sure your solution addresses every aspect listed in the problem (which is another reason not to bloat up the problem statement with a ton of small mini problems).

4. **Don't overengineer or oversolve the problem.** Once the problem is solved, stop. Don't add extra features or data or steps -- there are no bonus points or extra credit for doing more than what's required (and if you can't stop yourself, clarify that they are a v2).

5. **Don't solve problems that aren't listed.** If you can't directly trace a part of the solution back to a part of the problem, remove it and save it for a different P/S -- it's better to have 3 distinct but complementary P/S statements that can be independently discussed, than one mega P/S that gets bogged down in confusing detail.

A problem/solution statement is like stacking stones one atop the next. Make sure that every solution stone is fully supported by the problem stone beneath.

# Sectional Analysis

- **Proposal Name**: Evaluate the name of the proposal. Does it succinctly capture the core element of what is to be done? Is it phrased in an "active tone" (i.e., "Do X") rather than a "passive tone" (i.e., "X will happen")?

**Strategic Alignment**: Evaluate the strategy to make sure it is highlighting the subset that is relevant to the problem. Does it specifically relate the change to how we support our blue or red ocean strategies? Does it focus on a specific critical metric versus many ancillary metrics?

**Background**: Evaluate the background to make sure it is terse, factual, and relevant. Is there anything that can be removed without reducing comprehension of the problem or solution? Are there any opinions being presented as facts, or any facts that should be quantified?

**Problem Clarity**: Evaluate the clarity of the problem statement. Is it presented in a single, concise sentence following the recommended format? Does it avoid combining multiple issues? Does it express the problem from the perspective of the customer?

**Reverse Solution Trap**: Assess whether the problem statement hints at or includes the solution. If it does, identify the hints and explain.

**Solution Specificity**: Evaluate how specific and actionable the solution is. Does it directly address the problem? Is it detailed enough? Does it stay within the scope of the problem without going substantially beyond it? Does it address every part of the problem without missing anything?

**Hourglass Shape Compliance**: Assess whether the proposal follows the hourglass shape, with distinct Background, Problem, and Solution sections. Does each section serve its purpose without overstepping?

**Other Notes:** Please add anything else you note, whether about grammar, tone, the strength of the idea itself, etc.
